## S25 OrchestraBench

- Citation (verified on arXiv abs page): Yidian Chen, Yingzi Gu, Natan Vidra, Spurthi Setty, Sharon Zheng (all Anote). "OrchestraBench: Evaluating Multi-Agent Orchestration Failure Modes, Recovery, and Decomposition Quality." arXiv:2608.05263v1 [cs.AI], submitted 2026-08-05. 8 pages, 4 figures. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05263v1. Preprint. Ledger match: yes.
- Reading depth: full text (arXiv HTML, sections 1 to 8, Tables 1 to 8).
- What they did: They built a seeded failure-injection harness over templated workflows. They injected five failure modes from the MAST taxonomy into a verifiable arithmetic dependency chain run by one Claude agent (Sonnet 4.6), and measured recovery and cascade radius (downstream stages corrupted) by exact match to ground truth.
- What they found:
  - Section 5.2, Table 4 (N=150, n=30 per mode): final-task success was 1.00 for tool-invocation error, 0.30 for ambiguous delegation, and 0.00 for context pollution, conflicting outputs and premature action. The three latent modes had cascade radius 2.00.
  - Section 5.2: retry did not recover the latent modes. It "reproduces the error and only lengthens time-to-detection".
  - Section 5.3, Table 6 (N=750): latent cascade radius grew 0.93, 1.85, 2.80, 3.63, 4.67 for pipeline depths 3 to 7. The three catastrophic modes corrupt every downstream stage (depth minus 2).
  - Section 5.5, Table 8 (N=75): latent recovery was 0.08 for baseline policies, 0.83 for an LLM router given the trusted upstream value, and 1.00 for the oracle. Ablation: without the trusted upstream value, LLM latent recovery fell from 0.67 [0.46, 0.83] to 0.08 [0.00, 0.21] (paired drop 0.58, p = 5.19e-4, 24 pairs).
- Most relevant to our question:
  - FOR: latent faults (corrupted context, conflicting outputs, premature action) were never recovered by the agent and spread to every later stage; retry did not help (Table 4, Section 5.2).
  - FOR (this is the strongest match to our question): the repair gain in Section 5.5 came mostly from a trusted-state signal, not from the model detecting the fault itself. The authors say so: "mostly the trusted-upstream signal, not autonomous detection". This supports a check against state the agents do not control.
  - AGAINST (narrow): tool faults were fully recovered (1.00, Table 4) because the agent recomputed by hand. That is repair by the agent, but it relies on a visible tool error.
- Limitations that matter (Section 7): Experiments 2 and 3 run one Claude agent with prompt-level fault injection, not independent agents. The chain is arithmetic, so cascade radius is "partly by design". Workflows are synthetic. The trusted-state result is a probe, not a deployable estimate.
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: In a controlled single-agent chain, injected latent faults were never recovered and spread downstream, and repair worked mainly when given trusted upstream state.

## S23 MAS-FIRE

- Citation (verified on arXiv abs page): Jin Jia, Zhiling Deng, Zhuangbin Chen, Yingqi Wang, Zibin Zheng (Sun Yat-sen University). "MAS-FIRE: Fault Injection and Reliability Evaluation for LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems." arXiv:2602.19843v1 [cs.SE], submitted 2026-02-23. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19843v1. Preprint; the HTML shows an unfilled ACM conference template, so there is no venue. Ledger match: yes. Note: the HTML body spells the name "Fault Injection and Robustness Evaluation"; the arXiv title says "Reliability Evaluation".
- Reading depth: full text (arXiv HTML, sections 1 to 8). The paper has no separate limitations section.
- What they did: They defined 15 fault types (intra-agent and inter-agent) and injected them by prompt modification, response rewriting and message routing manipulation. They ran three existing systems (MetaGPT on HumanEval, Table-Critic on WikiTableQuestions, Camel on WebShop) with two models (GPT-5, DeepSeek-V3), single trial each (Table 1).
- What they found:
  - Section 5.1.2: under the "Blind Trust" fault (a system prompt says to accept another agent's output as true), MetaGPT's Robustness Score (share of originally solved tasks still solved) fell to 0.0%. Camel's success rate under Blind Trust was also 0.0% (Section 5.3.2). The authors say errors "propagate downstream without interception" in the linear pipeline.
  - Section 5.1.2 and 5.2: in Table-Critic, Blind Trust gave a Robustness Score of 6.32% with GPT-5 and 70.61% with DeepSeek-V3. In about 93.68% of GPT-5's failed cases the judge agent accepted the wrong reasoning. The authors attribute this to strict instruction following by the stronger model.
  - Section 5.1.1: Table-Critic's critique loop kept the Robustness Score above 89% under planning faults (about +45 points over MetaGPT's low of 43.84%). MetaGPT's shared message pool kept memory-fault Robustness Score above 90% (Camel about 67%).
  - Section 5.3.2: tool-call faults were recovered by system retries and error feedback (local recovery 100%, final success above 61%). Communication faults were handled by hard-coded rules (Robustness Score above 93% in MetaGPT).
  - Abstract: iterative closed-loop designs neutralise "over 40%" of faults that cause collapse in linear workflows. I did not find a table in the text that derives the 40% figure; cite it as the abstract's claim.
- Most relevant to our question:
  - FOR: when agents are told to trust a peer, or have no checker, one corrupted output passes downstream unchallenged; linear MetaGPT reached 0.0% under Blind Trust (Section 5.1.2).
  - AGAINST (with a qualifier): repair came from architecture, not from the peer agent's own judgement. A separate judge and refine loop, a shared message pool, retries on tool errors and rule-based filters did the repair (Findings 1, 3, 6, 7). The environment gives the error signal for tool faults.
  - CONTEXT: a stronger model did worse under Blind Trust (Section 5.2), so model capability does not replace an independent check. The authors suggest "environmental ground truth" and inline verification checkpoints as mitigations (Section 6.2); this is a suggestion, not a tested result.
- Limitations that matter: Faults are injected artificially, with one GPT-5-mini injector. Each task is run once (Pass@1), and three systems with two models are a small sample. There is no limitations section, so I report none from the authors.
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: In three tested systems, injected faults spread unchecked through a linear pipeline, while a separate critic loop, shared memory or retry rules limited the damage.

## S24 From Spark to Fire

- Citation (verified on arXiv HTML page): Yizhe Xie, Congcong Zhu, Xinyue Zhang, Tianqing Zhu, Dayong Ye, Minfeng Qi, Huajie Chen, Wanlei Zhou (City University of Macau; Minzu University of China). "From Spark to Fire: Modeling and Mitigating Error Cascades in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Collaboration." arXiv:2603.04474v2 [cs.MA], dated 2026-05-11. https://arxiv.org/html/2603.04474v2. Preprint. Ledger match: yes. The linked code is an anonymous.4open.science URL (I did not open it).
- Reading depth: full text of the main body, Sections I to VIII (appendix tables seen only where cited; Table X read).
- What they did: They model a multi-agent system as a directed graph and track how one injected false claim (a "seed") spreads, measured as the fraction of agents that adopt it. They tested six frameworks (LangChain, MetaGPT, AutoGen, CAMEL, CrewAI, LangGraph) and a message-layer governance plug-in that splits messages into atomic claims and checks them against a lineage graph.
- What they found:
  - Section IV-A: with one outdated-notice seed placed in the entry agent's instructions, five of six frameworks reached 100% final infection, "including settings with explicit reviewer or QA roles" (Table X: LangChain 89.2%, the others 100%).
  - Section IV-C, Table III: later correction costs more. Polluted rounds before intervention were 1.0, 2.9 and 3.9 for intervention at t=2, 4, 6.
  - Section VII-B, Table IV: a plain injected claim gave near-zero attack success (0 to 11.7%, except MetaGPT on Rigid tasks, 46.7%). A claim framed as "Compliance" or "Security FUD" raised attack success to 76.7 to 100% in most settings (CrewAI lower, 31.7 to 51.7% on the Compliance and FUD columns).
  - Section VII-C and Table VII: self-reflection (agent checks its own output) had an average BICR (Benign Infection Control Rate, 1 minus attack success) of 0.32. The governance layer gave 0.89 in Speed mode and 0.94 in Strict mode. Under Compliance, self-reflection fell to 5.0% on MetaGPT, 5.8% on AutoGen and 0.8% on LangGraph. The abstract's "at least 89%" matches the Speed average in Table VII; per-framework cells are lower in some cases (LangGraph 58.3 to 76.7%).
  - Section VII-C ablation (Table VI, Strict policy): removing quarantine or rollback left BICR at 3.1%, close to no defence (2.2%). Detection without enforcement did not help.
- Most relevant to our question:
  - FOR: a role-consistent false claim from an upstream agent was adopted and spread to the final artifact in nearly all runs, and reviewer or QA roles did not reliably stop it (Section IV-A, Table IV).
  - AGAINST (with a qualifier): the defence that worked is not the agents' own check. It is a layer outside the agents. Its trust boundary names only two trusted anchors: confirmed claims in the lineage graph, and "external evidence from designated verification tools" (Section VI-B). The agent-side self-check baseline did poorly. Note that their verification layer is "a self-built knowledge base and GPT-4o-mini" (Section VII-A), so the external anchor is itself an LLM-assisted check, not a ground-truth state.
  - CONTEXT: AGrail and CFG baselines cut infection but gave few usable outputs (Safe Completion 0.11 and 0.16, Table VII text).
- Limitations that matter (Section VIII): the spectral risk indicator is "a theoretical heuristic", not a calibrated predictor. The seeds are application-layer messages, and adaptive multi-stage adversaries are out of scope. Adoption is scored as binary. The defence adds latency and token cost (Strict mode 57,610 tokens per safe run, against 21,227 for Speed).
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: In six frameworks, one injected false claim usually spread to the final output, and a checker outside the agents contained it better than agent self-review.

## S32 Prompt Infection

- Citation (verified on arXiv HTML page): Donghyun Lee (University College London), Mo Tiwari (Stanford). "Prompt Infection: LLM-to-LLM Prompt Injection within Multi-Agent Systems." arXiv:2410.07283v1 [cs.MA], 2024-10-09. https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07283v1. Preprint; no venue shown on the page. Ledger match: yes.
- Reading depth: full text of the main body (Sections 1 to 8); the appendix with the infection prompts was not read.
- What they did: They built a prompt that copies itself from agent to agent (a "prompt infection"). They tested it in a simulated tool-using pipeline (reader, database, coder, writer agents; GPT-4o and GPT-3.5 Turbo; 120 instructions, Section 4.1) and in a simulated town of 10 to 50 agents (Section 4.2). They also tested prompt-based defences, including their own "LLM Tagging" (a marker naming the sending agent).
- What they found:
  - Section 5.1, global messaging (shared full history): self-replicating infection beat a non-replicating baseline on scam, malware and content tasks by 13.92% for GPT-4o and 209% for GPT-3.5. For data theft with more agents, the non-replicating baseline was better by an average 8.48%.
  - Section 5.1, local messaging (each agent sees only part of the history): self-replicating success was about 20% lower than in global messaging. The non-replicating baseline could not compromise more than two agents.
  - Section 5.1, RQ2: GPT-4o ignored 66% of self-replicating attacks and GPT-3.5 ignored 9%. Once compromised, GPT-4o executed the attack more precisely.
  - Section 5.2: in the agent town the infection spread in a logistic pattern. Spread depended on a manipulated memory importance score (Table 1: GPT-4o 1.94 without, 10.00 with manipulation). Without manipulation the infection died out (Figure 6b).
  - Section 6: LLM Tagging alone cut attack success by only 5%. Marking plus Tagging stopped all attacks in their tests; Instruction Defense plus Tagging left 3%; Sandwich plus Tagging left 16%. Marking alone still allowed 76% of attacks after the authors made a counterattack.
- Most relevant to our question:
  - FOR (with a qualifier): an agent that processes a peer's message as if it were an instruction can pass the compromise on; the "Data" note records what each agent adds as the prompt moves along (Section 3.1). This is about adversarial instruction injection, not honest false "done" claims.
  - AGAINST (weak): messaging design mattered. Local messaging lowered success by about 20%, and a model that ignored the attack stopped the chain at that agent (Section 5.1). Defences were prompt-level, not checks against external state.
  - CONTEXT: stronger models were harder to infect but more effective once infected (Section 5.1 RQ2).
- Limitations that matter (Section 7): only GPT-family models were tested in full; Claude tests were preliminary. Architectures were basic. Defences were tested against handcrafted attacks only. Injection into web content was simulated, not placed on live sites (Section 4.1).
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: In simulated pipelines, a self-copying prompt spread between agents, and the tested prompt-level defences worked only when combined.

## S36 Copies or Sources?

- Citation (verified on arXiv HTML page): Jianxin Gao, Runze Li, Tianyi Yu, Liangwei Ren, Bohan Chen, Zining Wang. "Copies or Sources? Measuring How LLM Aggregators Count Restated Evidence in Multi-Agent Systems." arXiv:2610.06192v1 [cs.AI], 2026-10-05. https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06192v1. Preprint; license CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. Ledger match: yes (author list and year agree).
- Reading depth: full text of the main body (Sections 1 to 7, Tables 1 to 2, Figures 3 to 5). Appendices A to G were not read except where the main text cites them.
- What they did: They gave LLM aggregators message logs in which the same reading is restated by forwarded copies. They measured a "copy weight" (0 = counts sources, 1 = counts every statement) against an exact Bayesian oracle, in three testbeds: controlled logs (1,000 items), web documents (500 items), and logs written by four-agent teams under four protocols (500 configurations). Four models from three providers were used.
- What they found:
  - Abstract and Section 4.1: a forwarded copy counts for 0.06 to 0.42 of a new reading (w3 = 0.06, 0.21, 0.27, 0.42 across the four models), even though provenance labels mark the records as copies. No model counted only sources, and none counted every statement.
  - Section 4.4: one reading stated three times crossed the early-commit boundary in 4.7%, 19.1%, 24.6% and 39.8% of logs by model. The oracle never commits on those logs. Abstract: 5% to 40%.
  - Section 4.6: in agent-written logs, 71% of messages from agents that never observed the reading restate it. Only 0.04% of messages claim the reading was verified. Pooled over protocols, 11.2% of one-observer logs would commit early.
  - Section 5: a one-paragraph declaration of what a copy contributes brought copy weight to 0.08 or less on controlled logs. A rule that tells agents to refer to readings instead of restating them cut early commitment from 11.2% to 1.1% and kept genuine corroboration (three-observer evidence 2.88 to 2.99 against a true-list reference of 2.90 to 2.96, Table 2). A note on how records are displayed raised the weight (0.27 to 0.39 readings for three models).
- Most relevant to our question:
  - FOR: aggregators partly counted restated peer claims as new evidence, so peer restatement inflated belief and moved decisions without new evidence (Sections 4.1, 4.4, 4.6). This is about counting repeated evidence, not a "step is done" claim, so it is an indirect precedent.
  - AGAINST (qualified): the repair was a structural change to the protocol or the aggregator's instructions, not a check against outside state. The reference rule worked because every reference could be resolved inside the log. The oracle itself is exact only because the test world has known readings.
  - CONTEXT: agents restated by default but almost never claimed verification (0.04%), so the over-counting came from repetition, not from false verification claims.
- Limitations that matter (Section 7): the controlled logs "make provenance honest and explicit": labels are correct, copies are exact, every reference resolves. Forged identifiers, relays that alter what they forward, and sources with shared failure modes are future work. The test world assumes independent sensors with stated reliabilities.
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: When messages restate one reading, LLM aggregators gave copies a partial weight and sometimes committed early; stating each reading once reduced this.

## S26 AgentDojo

- Citation (verified on arXiv abs and HTML pages): Edoardo Debenedetti, Jie Zhang, Mislav Balunovic, Luca Beurer-Kellner, Marc Fischer, Florian Tramer. "AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents." arXiv:2406.13352v3 [cs.CR], first submitted 2024-06-19, v3 dated 2024-11-24. https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13352v3. The arXiv page gives no venue. The HTML carries a data card and benchmark-track style sections, but I did not see a venue named; check before writing "NeurIPS". Ledger match: yes for title, authors and year.
- Reading depth: full text of Sections 1 to 5 (HTML). Appendices were not read.
- What they did: They built a stateful tool-calling environment with four suites (Workspace, Slack, Banking, Travel), 97 user tasks and 629 security test cases. Task success and attack success are scored by deterministic functions that inspect the environment state and the model output, not by an LLM judge (Sections 1, 3.1).
- What they found:
  - Section 1: current LLMs solve less than 66% of tasks with no attack. The authors say attacks succeed against the best agents in less than 25% of cases; a secondary attack detector drops attack success to 8%. Note: Table 2 gives a 45.8% baseline targeted success for the "Important message" attack on GPT-4o. I did not reconcile this with the "less than 25%" sentence; quote table values only with their table.
  - Section 4.1: more capable models tended to be easier to attack. Most models lose 10% to 25% absolute utility under attack. The Slack suite had a 92% attack success rate for GPT-4o. Injections near the end of a tool response worked best (up to 70% against GPT-4o).
  - Section 4.3: a "tool filter" (the agent restricts its tools before reading untrusted data) cut attack success to 7.5%. It fails for the 17% of test cases where the needed tools are enough to carry out the attack. All tested defences lost 15 to 20% of utility under attack (Figure 9). The detector had many false positives and cut utility.
- Most relevant to our question:
  - CONTEXT (design support): the success check runs on environment state ("formal utility checks computed over the environment state") and not on another model's judgement, because an injected attack could also fool an LLM judge (Section 3.1, which contrasts ToolEmu). This supports checking against state the agents do not control.
  - FOR (indirect): untrusted tool output can steer an agent into actions (Section 4.1). This is single-agent prompt injection, not agent-to-agent claims.
  - AGAINST (weak): structural isolation (the tool filter) beat prompt-level defences in this suite (Section 4.3), which points to structure outside the model, with the stated 17% gap.
- Limitations that matter (Sections 4.3, 5): attacks and defences are simple and general-purpose, and adaptive attackers are not covered. Tool filtering fails when the tool list cannot be planned ahead. Tasks and utility checks are written by hand, and the data are dummy data. Minor inconsistency: the text says 74 tools, the Table 1 caption says 70.
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: AgentDojo scores agent tasks and attacks by inspecting environment state, not by asking another model, and shows tool-output injection works against capable agents.

## S37 Evidence Laundering

- Citation (verified on the ACL Anthology page and the front page of the PDF): Ruihua Zhou, Hao Huang, Yijie Zheng, JiaTang Luo, Yunpeng Li, Yuling Liu, Dan Du. "Evidence Laundering: Deep Research Agents Mistake Correlated Sources for Independent Evidence." Proceedings of EMNLP 2026 (main), Budapest, pages 11682 to 11693. Anthology ID 2026.emnlp-main.509. https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.509/. Ledger match: yes.
- Reading depth: full text. The PDF was fetched and its text extracted with a stdlib script (some spaces and Greek symbols were lost, so I read numbers only where the text was clear). Figures were not viewed; figure values come from text and table extraction.
- What they did: They built a synthetic corpus of 100 claims about invented entities and compared three source structures built from the same false claim: one false page (A), five paraphrased pages with a shared template and publisher (C, the "laundered" cluster), and five pages written in different styles with no shared template (F, an "apparent independence" control). A ReAct agent with BM25 retrieval gave a credence and a publish or abstain decision. They then tested a post-retrieval "provenance gate" that collapses pages with a shared writing skeleton.
- What they found:
  - Section 6.1, Table 1 (primary model gpt-5.4, n=90): false-publish rate was 0.71 for A, 0.83 for C and 0.82 for F. Credence rose by +0.81 log10 odds from A to C. The Dependence Discount Index (DDI; 0 = cluster discounted to one source, 1 = counted like independent sources) was 1.00 [bootstrap interval shown as 0.75 to 1.x, cut off in my extraction]. The C to F credence gap was +0.08, which a two one-sided test (TOST) judged equivalent within 0.15 log odds.
  - Section 6.2, Table 2: credence rose from A to C in all eight model settings. DDI ranged from 0.71 (claude-opus-4.1) to 1.01 (grok-3).
  - Section 6.3: most of the credence gain came when the cluster grew from one page to three (+0.78 at k=3; +0.78 to +0.81 for k=3, 5, 7, 10).
  - Section 6.4, Table 5 (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, top-5 retrieval): adding five correct pages did not help. False publication stayed 0.76 under C and 0.72 under F, against 0.00 under A. Correct pages opened averaged 3.48 under A and 0.72 and 0.64 under C and F. With top-12 retrieval and eight opens, false publication fell to 0.00 in all three.
  - Section 7.2, Table 7: the masked-skeleton gate cut effective sources in C from 5.00 to 1.03 and kept 4.94 for F. DDI fell from 1.00 to a magnitude of 0.39 (the sign was lost in my text extraction, so check the PDF before quoting; a negative value would mean over-discounting). Word-shingle deduplication also merged the independent controls (F and E fell to 2.00).
  - Section 7.4, Table 10: when five false pages were written independently (17 valid clusters), the gate kept 4.41 effective sources in C against 4.88 in F; recall was 0.0765 and 11 of 17 clusters were not collapsed.
- Most relevant to our question:
  - FOR: agreement among several pages was treated almost as independent confirmation. The agents did not discount same-origin pages (DDI near 1.0, Table 1). This is a retrieval and corroboration setting, not agent-to-agent "task done" claims.
  - AGAINST (qualified): a gate outside the agent, on observable page structure, cut the effect in the matched test (Table 7). It failed once the pages were written independently (Table 10), so it detects shared form, not shared origin, and the authors say it does not have access to true provenance ("true authorship, ground truth provenance, and truth labels are unavailable at test time", Section 2).
- Limitations that matter (Limitations section): the sandbox does not reproduce a live search environment. The natural-news test measures metadata-based provenance classification, not agent behaviour end to end (recall 0.359, Table 9). The static gate rarely collapses independently generated pages. The decision effect depends partly on which documents the retriever exposes.
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: In a controlled test, deep research agents gave five paraphrased copies of one false claim about the same credence as five independently written pages.
- Side note for the ledger: this paper's reference list cites AgentDojo as NeurIPS 2024, volume 37, pages 82895 to 82920 (Debenedetti et al. 2024). That is a secondhand venue clue for S26; I did not verify it at the NeurIPS source.

## S38 Trust Cascade through Provenance Erasure

- Citation (verified on the ACL Anthology page and the PDF front page): Yuxin Zhou, Xiao Ding, Tianle Chang, Ye He, Wenbin Zhang, Shangzhan Li, Yijia Meng, Wenyu Zang, Bibo Cai, Qi Shi (Harbin Institute of Technology; CETC Academy of Cyber; Tsinghua). "Trust Cascade through Provenance Erasure: How Multi-Agent Delegation Breaks Single-Agent Safety." Proceedings of EMNLP 2026 (main), Budapest, pages 23130 to 23148. Anthology ID 2026.emnlp-main.1005. https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1005/. Ledger match: yes.
- Reading depth: full main text (Sections 1 to 6, Tables 2, 3, 6, 7, Limitations) and Appendix Q. Other appendices were not read. The PDF text was extracted with a stdlib script, which drops en-dashes, so "33-63%" appears as "3363%" in the raw text. I read it as 33 to 63%, which agrees with the per-model values in Section 3.5.
- What they did: They used a two-agent leader and follower setup. An injected instruction enters through the leader's tool observation. The leader writes a delegation in natural language, and the follower acts on it. They measured how many trust markers survive the delegation, and how often the follower complies. A control replays the leader's delegation text to a single agent as a user prompt.
- What they found:
  - Section 3.3, Table 2 (460 injection and delegation pairs): the delegation text lost 98% of format markers, 100% of authority entities and 44% of factual entities (56% of email addresses survived); combined 80%. The task payload often survived.
  - Section 3.4, Table 3: the follower's "Collapse Index" (0 = perfect channel discrimination, 1 = none) was 0.376 for direct injection and 0.836 for delegated instructions (difference +0.460, non-overlapping intervals).
  - Section 3.5: system-channel direct injection gave 0% single-agent attack success (270 cells). The cascade gave 63.3% (Gemma), 43.3% (Qwen) and 33.3% (Llama). Abstract: 0% to 33 to 63% across eight open-weight models.
  - Section 3.4 control: Qwen followers complied 81.8% on the replayed delegation text against 83.3% on the raw injection through the same user channel (difference -1.5 points). The authors read this as channel trust escalation, not reformulation of content.
  - Sections 5.3 to 5.5: a rule-based Trust Boundary Auditor (TBA) flagged 86.11% to 100% of delegations that passed leader filtering, with 0% false positives across 1,132 cells (Section 5.3); unconditional detection was 58.40%. A provenance-declaration schema reached 0% attack success but caused 44.4% format failures on benign delegations (Table 7). TBA alone left 5.6% attack success on the three held-out scenarios.
  - Section 5.6: an adversary who knows the rules can evade TBA with entity-free or alias-based payloads.
- Most relevant to our question:
  - FOR (strongest match among these papers to a hand-off claim): when one agent passes work to the next, the receiver sees the leader's rewritten message as a trusted peer instruction, and the original source markers are gone (Tables 2 and 3). This is about trust in a peer's message after a hand-off, though the payload is an injected instruction, not an honest "step done" report.
  - AGAINST (qualified): a check at the hand-off boundary that compares the delegation with the user task and the tool observations (TBA) caught most cascades that passed the leader. It compares text, not real-world state, and is evadable (Section 5.6). Leader-side filtering also blocked some attacks (Section 3.2).
  - CONTEXT: stronger models interrupted different cascade stages but did not remove the boundary problem (Abstract, Section 6).
- Limitations that matter (Limitations, Appendix Q): single-step, two-agent cascade only; multi-hop chains and production systems are untested. The replay control is clean only for the reflective-leader (Qwen) regime. Cell counts per model in the control are unbalanced (44, 28, 60). Scenarios are synthetic with simulated tools. Internal note: Appendix Q says TBA was not compared with structured delegation formats, but Section 5.5 and Table 7 include a JSON-schema arm and a provenance-schema arm; cite Table 7 with that inconsistency in mind.
- Safe one-sentence summary for the paper: In a two-agent test, delegation stripped source markers from injected instructions, and followers then complied with them far more often than with the raw injection.
